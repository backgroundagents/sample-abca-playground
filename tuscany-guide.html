Tuscany:
Italy's Renaissance heartland

9 min read  ·  One-week itinerary

Rolling hills striped with cypress trees, medieval hilltop towns wrapped in golden stone, and some of the finest art and architecture ever created by human hands — Tuscany is the landscape that defined the Western imagination of Italy, and it has lost none of its power to enchant.

Why Tuscany?

Tuscany occupies a singular place in European culture. This is the region that gave the world the Renaissance — Florence alone contains more masterpieces per square metre than most countries possess in their entirety. But Tuscany's appeal extends far beyond its museums: the countryside is a living canvas of vineyards, olive groves, and stone farmhouses that has changed remarkably little since the Medici era.

The region rewards slow travel above all. A week spent moving between Florence, Siena, and the smaller hill towns — with long lunches in village trattorias, afternoons in quiet churches, and evenings watching the light turn the landscape amber — is one of the great European journeys. This is not a place to rush.

Practical tip

A car is essential for exploring the Tuscan countryside between towns. Roads are well-maintained and scenic, but fuel stations can be scarce in rural areas. Book accommodation with parking — finding a spot in hilltop towns like San Gimignano or Montepulciano without a reserved space is near-impossible in season. Alternatively, base yourself in Florence or Siena and take day trips by bus.

The cities & towns

Tuscany's urban centres range from the global art capital of Florence to perfectly preserved medieval towns that seem untouched by time. Siena's shell-shaped Piazza del Campo is one of Europe's most beautiful public spaces; San Gimignano's skyline of medieval towers rises from the surrounding vineyards like a stone forest; and Lucca's intact Renaissance walls now serve as an elevated garden promenade circling the old city.

Florence

The birthplace of the Renaissance and home to the Uffizi, the Duomo, and Michelangelo's David. Overwhelming in the best sense — allow at least three full days and book major museums in advance.

3 nights recommended

Siena

Florence's historic rival, with a magnificent Gothic cathedral and the fan-shaped Piazza del Campo — site of the Palio horse race held twice each summer. More intimate and walkable than Florence.

2 nights recommended

San Gimignano

The "Medieval Manhattan" — a tiny walled town crowned by 14 surviving stone towers, once a symbol of rival families' wealth. Best explored early morning before the day-trip buses arrive.

Day trip or 1 night

Val d'Orcia

The iconic Tuscan landscape of rolling hills, lone cypress trees, and golden light — a UNESCO World Heritage site. Base yourself in Pienza or Montepulciano and drive the winding roads between villages.

2 nights recommended

When to go

April through June and September through October are ideal — warm days, manageable crowds, and the landscape at its most photogenic. Spring brings wildflowers to the hillsides; autumn delivers harvest season, with grape-picking in Chianti and truffle hunting in the forests south of Siena. The light in both seasons is extraordinary — soft, golden, and seemingly designed for photography.

July and August are hot (often exceeding 35°C in Florence) and crowded, though the countryside remains more pleasant than the cities. Winter is quiet and atmospheric — Florence's museums are blissfully uncrowded, and the hill towns take on a moody, cinematic quality in the mist.

Where to stay

Tuscany offers everything from converted medieval monasteries and aristocratic villas to working wine estates with guest rooms. In Florence, stay in the Oltrarno neighbourhood for a more local atmosphere away from the tourist centre. In the countryside, an agriturismo (farm stay) is the quintessential Tuscan experience — stone buildings among olive groves, often with a pool and home-cooked dinners using the estate's own produce. Castello di Ama, Borgo San Felice, and Rosewood Castiglion del Bosco represent the luxury end.

Budget guide

Tuscany ranges from affordable to extravagant. Simple agriturismos and guesthouses start at €80–120 per night; boutique hotels in Florence and Siena run €200–400. Meals at village trattorias cost €20–35 per person; Michelin-starred dining ranges from €80–200. Daily budget excluding accommodation: €50–80 for modest spending; €120–200 for the full experience including wine tastings and museum entries.

Food & wine

Tuscan cuisine is famously simple — built on extraordinary ingredients treated with restraint. Bistecca alla fiorentina (a thick-cut T-bone from Chianina cattle, grilled over charcoal) is the region's signature dish. Ribollita (a hearty bread and vegetable soup), pici (hand-rolled thick pasta), and crostini with chicken liver are staples of every traditional trattoria. The bread is unsalted — a centuries-old tradition that initially surprises but makes perfect sense alongside the region's bold, salty cured meats.

The wines need little introduction: Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino, and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano are among Italy's greatest reds. Many estates welcome visitors for tastings — Antinori nel Chianti Classico and Castello Banfi are architecturally stunning; smaller family producers like Isole e Olena and Il Poggione offer more intimate experiences.
