Edinburgh:
Scotland's ancient capital

8 min read  ·  Four-day itinerary

A city built on volcanic hills, where a medieval Old Town of narrow closes and towering tenements faces a Georgian New Town of classical elegance across a green valley. Edinburgh is a place of two halves — ancient and Enlightenment, brooding stone and wide crescents — and both are magnificent.

Why Edinburgh?

Edinburgh is one of Europe's most dramatic cities. The castle sits on an extinct volcano, presiding over a Royal Mile that cascades downhill through a thousand years of Scottish history to the Palace of Holyroodhouse. Arthur's Seat — a wild, craggy peak — rises from the city centre itself, offering hill walks with views to the Firth of Forth and the Highlands beyond. Few capitals wear their geology so proudly.

Beyond its natural theatre, Edinburgh is a city of ideas. The Scottish Enlightenment was born here — Hume, Smith, and Hutton walked these streets — and that intellectual tradition lives on in the world's largest arts festival every August, in its thriving literary scene, and in a university whose buildings are woven into the urban fabric. Add world-class museums, a food scene transformed in the last decade, and some of the finest whisky bars on earth, and you have a city that rewards weeks of exploration.

Practical tip

Edinburgh is a walking city, but it is also a hilly one. Wear sturdy shoes — the cobblestones of the Old Town and the paths up Arthur's Seat demand them. The city is compact: you can walk from the Castle to Holyrood in twenty minutes, and from the Old Town to the shore at Leith in forty.

The neighbourhoods

The Old Town clings to the ridge between the Castle and Holyrood, its tenements rising six or seven storeys above wynds and closes that tunnel down to the Cowgate below. This is medieval Edinburgh at its most atmospheric — dark, vertical, and full of hidden courtyards. The Royal Mile is the spine, but the real discoveries lie off it: Victoria Street curving downhill in painted shopfronts, the Grassmarket's open square beneath the castle rock, and the quiet cloisters of Greyfriars Kirk.

The New Town, to the north, is anything but new — it dates from the 1760s — but its wide streets, grand crescents, and neoclassical architecture represent a complete contrast to the medieval tangle across the valley. Princes Street offers views back to the castle; George Street and Queen Street hold galleries, restaurants, and bars. Stockbridge, at the New Town's northern edge, has a village atmosphere with independent shops and a Sunday market. Leith, the old port two miles north, has reinvented itself as the city's food and drink quarter.

Edinburgh Castle

Perched on Castle Rock, the fortress has dominated the city's skyline for nine centuries. The crown jewels of Scotland, the Stone of Destiny, and panoramic views over the city make this an essential first stop.

Half-day visit

Arthur's Seat

An 822-foot volcanic peak in the heart of the city. The climb takes about 45 minutes from Holyrood and rewards with sweeping views of Edinburgh, the Forth, and on clear days, the Highlands.

Morning hike

The Royal Mile

The ancient road from Castle to Palace, lined with closes, kirks, and centuries of history. St Giles' Cathedral, the Real Mary King's Close, and countless pubs and whisky shops line the route.

Full day on foot

Leith

Edinburgh's historic port turned foodie destination. Michelin-starred restaurants sit alongside traditional pubs and the Royal Yacht Britannia. The waterfront is atmospheric at any time of day.

Afternoon & evening

When to go

August is festival season — the Edinburgh International Festival, the Fringe, the Book Festival, and the Royal Edinburgh Military Tattoo transform the city into a global stage. It is exhilarating but crowded and expensive; book accommodation months ahead. May, June, and September offer long daylight hours (sunset after 9pm in June), mild weather, and far fewer visitors.

Winter brings short days but a magical atmosphere: the city's stone buildings glow under low light, Christmas markets fill Princes Street Gardens, and Hogmanay (New Year's Eve) is celebrated with a street party famous the world over. Expect temperatures of 2–7°C and frequent rain — but also roaring fires in ancient pubs and whisky to warm the coldest evening.

Where to stay

The Old Town places you at the heart of the action — atmospheric hotels in converted tenements, steps from the Royal Mile and the city's best restaurants. The New Town offers Georgian elegance: boutique hotels in townhouses on quiet crescents, with easy access to galleries and shopping. Stockbridge suits those who prefer a village feel within walking distance of the centre. Leith is ideal for food-focused visitors and offers better value, with the tram connecting you to the centre in fifteen minutes.

Budget guide

Edinburgh is moderate by northern European standards outside festival season, when prices spike. Hostels start at £20–35 per night; mid-range hotels run £100–180. A pub lunch costs £10–15; dinner at a good restaurant £30–55 per person. Daily budget excluding accommodation: £40–70 for modest spending; £80–150 for the full experience including whisky tastings and entrance fees. In August, expect to pay 50–100% more for accommodation.

Food & drink

Edinburgh's food scene has been transformed in the last decade. The city now boasts multiple Michelin-starred restaurants, but the real joy is in the breadth of quality at every level. Traditional dishes are having a renaissance: haggis, neeps, and tatties served with modern flair; Cullen skink (smoked haddock chowder) in cosy pubs; and fresh seafood from the Scottish coast — langoustines, oysters, and hand-dived scallops — at Leith's waterfront restaurants.

Whisky is Edinburgh's great drink. The city has dozens of specialist whisky bars offering hundreds of single malts, from peaty Islays to honeyed Speysides. The Scotch Whisky Experience on the Royal Mile provides an excellent introduction. Craft beer has also flourished, and Edinburgh's gin distilleries — a nod to the city's historic gin production — offer tastings and tours.
